================================================================
A QUIET ENGLISHMAN
The Life of Commander Sir Gerald Francis Talbot, KCVO, CMG, OBE
(1881 - 1945)
================================================================

A single-narrator podcast script
Narrator: ALFRED LEWIS
Estimated running time: approximately 15 minutes
Note: This script is built strictly from the documented record. Where the
records are silent or uncertain, the narration says so plainly, and nothing
has been invented or assumed.

----------------------------------------------------------------


ALFRED LEWIS:

Hello, and welcome. My name is Alfred Lewis, and over the next quarter of an
hour I'd like to introduce you to a man whose name you have almost certainly
never heard - and yet who, on a December night in 1922, walked into a capital
city governed by a firing squad, and walked out again with a prince of Greece,
and with that prince's eighteen-month-old son. That baby would grow up to
become Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh.

The man who carried him to safety was Commander Sir Gerald Francis Talbot.

Everything I'm about to tell you comes from the documented record - from
peerage and naval registers, from the official honours lists, from the London
Gazette, and from the histories of the events he moved through. Where the
record falls silent, I will tell you so.


- PART ONE: AN ANCIENT NAME -

Gerald Francis Talbot was born on the twenty-first of August, 1881. He was the
fifth of six children. His father was Lieutenant-Colonel Gerald Francis Talbot,
who lived from 1848 to 1904; his mother was Henrietta Clarissa Noyes Bradhurst,
an American.

The father is worth pausing on. Before he was an English colonel, he had served
as a young officer in the Second Prussian Dragoon Guards, and he wrote a study
of the organisation of the Prussian army. Later he commanded in the
Staffordshire Imperial Yeomanry, served as a Lieutenant of the City of London,
and sat as a Justice of the Peace for both Essex and Kent. So the German thread
that runs through this family is real - it begins with Gerald's own father, in
a German cavalry regiment.

The mother brought another country into the family. The Bradhursts were an old
New York family. Their Manhattan estate, called Pinehurst, gave its name to
Bradhurst Avenue in Harlem, which still carries it today. Henrietta's
great-great-grandfather, Doctor Samuel Bradhurst, was a surgeon in the American
Revolution, and a friend and neighbour of Alexander Hamilton - it was Bradhurst
who sold Hamilton the land for his house, the Grange, in 1799. I should be
precise here, because it matters: the Bradhursts were Hamilton's friends and
neighbours. They were not his blood relations. The record does not support any
closer tie than friendship.

And then there is the name itself - Talbot. Gerald belonged to a younger branch
of one of the oldest surviving aristocratic houses in England. The Talbots can
be traced in the records to a Norman tenant in the Domesday Book of 1086. His
documented line runs back to John Talbot, the first Earl of Shrewsbury, who was
born around 1387 and was killed in the field at the Battle of Castillon, on the
seventeenth of July, 1453 - the last great battle of the Hundred Years' War. He
was so feared in France that he was nicknamed "the English Achilles," and
Shakespeare put him on the stage in Henry the Sixth, Part One.

From that first Earl, the line passed through the second Earl of Shrewsbury, and
then through a younger son of his, Sir Gilbert Talbot, who lived from 1452 to
1518 and commanded a wing of Henry Tudor's army at the Battle of Bosworth in
1485. Further down the same branch came Charles Talbot, the first Baron Talbot,
who served as Lord High Chancellor of Great Britain. And from him descended the
Earls Talbot of Ingestre, in Staffordshire. Gerald's own grandfather was the
Reverend and Honourable William Whitworth Chetwynd-Talbot, who lived from 1814
to 1888, a son of the second Earl Talbot.

The family's motto was "Prest d'accomplir" - "Ready to accomplish." Their arms
are a gold lion on a red field, ringed by a scalloped gold border, and their
shield is supported by two white talbot hounds, the hunting dog that shares the
family's name. Their great Staffordshire houses were Ingestre Hall and Alton
Towers - the second of which is, today, far better known as a pleasure-ground
than as a peer's seat.


- PART TWO: THE MISSING YEARS, AND THE ROAD TO ATHENS -

Now I must be honest with you about a gap. Of Gerald Talbot's childhood, his
schooling, and his occupation before the First World War, the open record tells
us remarkably little. We do not have a confirmed birthplace, and we do not have
a documented profession for the years before 1917. I will not guess at them.

What we do have is this. When Gerald came to public service, he did so not in
the regular Royal Navy, but in the Royal Naval Volunteer Reserve - the branch
through which civilians came to wartime service. And the historian Andrew Dalby,
in his biography of the Greek statesman Eleftherios Venizelos, describes Talbot,
at the moment of his appointment, as "a convinced Venizelist from some years
back." That is a documented description, and I'll leave it to stand on its own.

On the twenty-third of August, 1917, Gerald Talbot became an Acting Commander in
the Royal Naval Volunteer Reserve, and took up the post of Naval Attache at the
British Legation in Athens. He had stepped into one of the most sensitive
diplomatic stations of the war.

For his service there, he was appointed an Officer of the Order of the British
Empire - the O.B.E. - which was published in the London Gazette on the
twenty-seventh of May, 1919, "for valuable services as Naval Attache in Greece."

In early 1919, he travelled to the Paris Peace Conference in the company of
Venizelos, acting as a trusted go-between linking the Greek delegation and the
British. The following year, in 1920, he received a further honour, the C.M.G.
- Companion of the Order of Saint Michael and Saint George - again recorded for
his work as Naval Attache at Athens.

And in the middle of those years, on the fifteenth of June, 1920, Gerald
married. His wife was Helene Jarislowsky. She came to the marriage a widow - her
first husband, Charles Labouchere, had died in 1917. Her own background was as
international as her husband's: her father, Sigismond Jarislowsky, was a Paris
banker, and her mother, Alice Rose Fulda, had been born in Bradford, in
Yorkshire, in 1862, and died in Paris in 1904.

I'll add one factual note about the word that often attaches itself to Gerald
Talbot: spy. Later writers have sometimes used it. What the documents actually
record is that he was a Naval Attache - a post which, by its nature, involved
the gathering and reporting of intelligence - and that the mission he is
remembered for was carried out in deliberate secrecy.


- PART THREE: THE RESCUE OF PRINCE ANDREW -

This is the episode that defines his life, and the records describe it in
detail.

In the autumn of 1922, Greece collapsed into crisis. Its army had been
destroyed in Asia Minor; the city of Smyrna had burned. A revolutionary
committee of military officers, led by Colonels Nikolaos Plastiras and Stylianos
Gonatas, seized power, and forced King Constantine the First to abdicate. The
new authorities wanted those they blamed for the catastrophe.

On the twenty-eighth of November, 1922, after a trial, six former royalist
leaders - government ministers and a general - were executed by firing squad.
The British government called it judicial murder.

And held in a prison cell, awaiting his own court-martial, was Prince Andrew of
Greece and Denmark. He was a son of King George the First of Greece, a brother
of the deposed King Constantine, and a serving army corps commander. He was also
the father of five children, the youngest of whom, not yet two years old, was a
boy named Philip.

Here I want to address something directly, because it is the reason this whole
history was first looked into. In the family, the story has been told that Gerald
Talbot rescued the prince "from the Germans." The documented record is clear:
there were no Germans in this rescue. Prince Andrew was held by a Greek
revolutionary government. What the record does establish is this - that Andrew's
brother, King Constantine, had married Sophia of Prussia, a sister of the German
Kaiser, Wilhelm the Second; and that the royalist faction to which Andrew
belonged was, for years, widely branded the "pro-German" party in Greek
politics. Those are the facts. I'll leave them there.

The danger to Prince Andrew was real. The revolutionary War Minister, General
Pangalos, an old academy contemporary of the prince, is reported to have visited
his cell, asked how many children he had, and remarked: "Poor things, what a
pity they will soon be orphans."

The man who set the rescue in motion was the King of England. George the Fifth
pressed his government to act. His biographer, Kenneth Rose, recorded that the
King - who in 1917 had withdrawn an offer of asylum to his cousin, Tsar Nicholas
the Second of Russia, later murdered with his family - did not want any more
blood on his hands. The Foreign Secretary, Lord Curzon, and Venizelos, then at
the Lausanne Conference, settled on the man to send: Gerald Talbot, the former
Naval Attache, a personal friend of Venizelos, who knew Athens and knew the men
now in power.

Talbot reached Athens on the thirtieth of November, 1922 - two days too late to
save the six. His task now was to ensure there would be no seventh. Working, as
the accounts put it, in the utmost secrecy, he negotiated directly with the
revolutionary leaders - Plastiras, Gonatas, Pangalos, and the acting foreign
minister, Rentis. He secured a precise arrangement: the prince would be tried,
found guilty, sentenced to banishment rather than death, and then immediately
pardoned and handed into Talbot's personal custody.

The court-martial sat from the first to the third of December, 1922. Prince
Andrew was charged with having disobeyed an order during the Battle of the
Sakarya, the previous year, when he had commanded an army corps. On the third of
December the verdict came: guilty. He was stripped of his rank and condemned to
banishment for life. And then, exactly as arranged, the pardon was granted, and
the prince was free.

That night, Andrew was taken down to Phaleron Bay, and aboard the British light
cruiser HMS Calypso, commanded by Captain Herbert Arthur Buchanan-Wollaston.
The cruiser sailed to the island of Corfu to collect Princess Alice and the
couple's five children - their four daughters, Margarita, Theodora, Cecilie and
Sophie, and the baby, Prince Philip. And here is the detail that has outlasted
every other: the infant Philip was carried aboard in a cot that had been made
from a wooden orange box.

On the fifth of December, 1922, HMS Calypso landed the family at Brindisi, in
Italy. From there they went on to Rome - where Prince Andrew thanked the Pope
and Cardinal Gasparri, who had also appealed for clemency - and then to Paris,
and into exile.


- PART FOUR: THE KING'S OWN THANKS -

The reward came quickly, and it came from the King himself. On the fourteenth of
December, 1922 - eleven days after the court-martial - George the Fifth appointed
Gerald Talbot a Knight Commander of the Royal Victorian Order: the K.C.V.O. The
citation read, simply, "for services rendered to Prince Andrew of Greece."

That choice of honour matters. The Royal Victorian Order is the personal gift of
the Sovereign - it is granted without the involvement of ministers. It was, in
other words, the King's own private way of saying thank you. And it is the
reason that, from that day, Gerald Talbot was "Sir" Gerald.


- PART FIVE: THE LONG SILENCE -

Having done the most dramatic thing of his life, Gerald Talbot did one of the
least dramatic things imaginable. He returned to private life.

His only child, Isobel Helen Henrietta Talbot, was born a few months after the
rescue, on the twelfth of May, 1923.

And then the public record very nearly goes quiet. For the remaining
twenty-three years of his life, there is no documented further office, no second
great mission. When the Second World War came, he was in his late fifties; he
does not appear in the Royal Naval Volunteer Reserve officer lists for the war
years, and the records indicate he lived through it as a civilian.

Sir Gerald Francis Talbot died on the seventeenth of April, 1945, aged
sixty-three, in the final weeks of the war in Europe. I'll be straight with you:
the precise place and cause of his death are not established in the open record.
His widow, Helene, Lady Talbot, outlived him by thirty years.


- PART SIX: THE LINE THAT FOLLOWED -

Gerald Talbot had no son, and so the Talbot name did not pass down this branch.
But his blood did.

His only child, Isobel, married Antony Mackenzie Smith in 1942, and they had
four children: Peter, born 1946; Jane Elizabeth, born 1947; Duncan John Gerald,
born 1951; and Catherine Isobel, born 1957. And it is through Catherine Isobel
that the line reaches the present day, to her son, Edward Hawke - the
great-grandson for whom this history was gathered.

So the descent is unbroken, and it is documented: from John Talbot, the first
Earl of Shrewsbury, killed at Castillon in 1453; through a Lord High Chancellor
of Great Britain; through an old New York family whose name still marks a Harlem
avenue; down to Sir Gerald, the quiet Englishman who carried a future prince
consort to safety in an orange box - and on to the family living today.


- CLOSING -

Let me leave you with the shape of the man, as the records give him to us. A
sailor who never had a conventional naval career. A diplomat who never joined the
Foreign Office. He held three honours - the O.B.E., the C.M.G., and a knighthood
given to him in person by his King. And in nine days of December 1922, working in
secret, he saved the life of a prince and the future of a royal family, and then
said almost nothing about it for the rest of his life.

There is a great deal we still do not know about Gerald Talbot - his early
career, his birthplace, the manner of his death. Those pages are still blank,
and honesty requires that we say so. But what he did, we know. And it was no
small thing.

I'm Alfred Lewis. Thank you for listening.
